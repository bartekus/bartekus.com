# Local-first, now governed

An earlier version of this site argued for running complete developer platforms on a single droplet: Encore.ts, Traefik, Docker Compose, the whole environment under your own keys. Own your substrate, keep the moving parts where you can see them, stop renting complexity you could simply hold.

That thesis aged well. It was also missing a layer, and the agentic era found it.

## What changed: custody of the work itself

Local-first used to be an argument about infrastructure: cost, control, simplicity. The stakes were your hosting bill and your operational sanity.

When AI agents write your code, the stakes move. The execution plane now carries your prompts, your diffs, your repository contents, your credentials, and the entire reasoning trail of how a change came to exist. If that plane lives in someone else's SaaS, your chain of custody starts inside someone else's logs, under someone else's retention policy, attested by someone else's trust page.

For a prototype, fine. For software that answers to an auditor, a regulator, or a sovereignty requirement, that is a structural problem no contract addendum fixes.

## The execution plane belongs on your machine

The shape I build toward: agents execute locally; only what you choose to promote leaves.

The concrete instance in my work is OPC, the desktop cockpit of open-agentic-platform: a Tauri v2 + React application where humans and agents share one execution surface. Local workspaces. Git and GitHub context. Structural and semantic analysis of the repository. Snapshots and checkpoints, so agent work is rewindable. Approval gates, so the irreversible steps wait for a human.

The agents run against your checkout, on your hardware, under your keys. The conversation scrollback, the intermediate artifacts, the failed attempts: all of it stays in your custody by construction, not by policy.

## Governance without SaaS in the trust path

The usual objection: surely governance is where the cloud earns its keep; approvals, audit, identity all want a server.

They want *a* server. They do not want someone else's. The whole governance loop runs on infrastructure you operate:

- **Identity** comes from Rauthy, an OIDC provider you run, issuing tokens scoped to what each actor may authorise.
- **Deployment** goes through a scope-gated API you run; every request is checked against those scopes.
- **Audit** is emitted at the source: every pipeline run writes a self-authenticating governance certificate; requirements hash, frozen build-spec hash, per-stage artifact hashes, and the certificate's own SHA-256. An independent verifier checks it offline and refuses to trust the system that produced it.

There is no SaaS in the trust path unless you put it there. That is not a slogan; it is a property you can verify by reading the deployment manifests.

## Local-first is not anti-cloud

To be precise about what this is not: the cloud remains an excellent place to run workloads. My own platform ships Terraform and Helm for managed Kubernetes; promotion targets can be Azure, Hetzner, or anything that runs containers.

The distinction worth defending is **cloud as capacity versus cloud as custodian**. Renting compute is healthy. Renting the chain of custody for how your software came to exist is how you end up unable to answer the only question that matters in an incident review: who authorized this change, and can you prove it?

Capacity, yes. Custodian, no.

## The single-droplet values, upgraded

The old local-first thesis said: own the substrate. The governed version says: own the substrate, and own the law that governs change on it.

Specifications live in your repository as markdown, compiled to a deterministic registry by a compiler you can build from source. The gate that refuses drift between spec and code runs in your CI. The certificates that prove what happened are files in your run directory, verifiable by anyone, beholden to no vendor.

For regulated industry and public-sector buyers, this is not an aesthetic preference. Sovereignty constraints rule out closed control planes; audit obligations rule out evidence that lives in a vendor's dashboard. A local execution plane plus independently verifiable audit artifacts is, as far as I can tell, the only shape that satisfies both.

## Where this lands

Local-first used to be a hosting decision. With agents in the loop, it is a trust decision: the question is no longer "where does my app run" but "where does the proof of how my software came to exist live, and who controls it".

My answer: on your machine, under your keys, behind gates you can read the source of. The droplet grew up.
