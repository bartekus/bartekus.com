# The factory is a service now

Of the four repositories I [archived this week](/writing/from-open-agentic-platform-to-statecraft), the one I was fondest of was factory-encore. It had the cleanest idea: separate the *process* of building software (requirements, design, specification) from the *implementation* (frameworks, languages, patterns) by placing a formal contract between them. Business documents went in; a frozen, technology-free Build Specification came out; an adapter turned the specification into a running application. The process layer never named a framework. Add a stack by adding an adapter.

I still believe every word of that idea. I archived the repo anyway, because the idea was wearing the wrong product around it, and it took shipping the successor to see the mistake clearly.

## Nobody installs a factory

factory-encore was a framework: a thing you would clone, configure, and operate yourself to stamp your own applications. Its sibling template-encore was the runnable baseline the factory composed from, in a separate repository, held in sync by a cross-repo lockstep protocol so the generator could never silently diverge from the baseline it targeted.

Notice what that architecture assumes: a customer who wants to *own a factory*. That customer barely exists. Organizations do not want factories; they want the things factories produce, arriving with the paperwork that proves how they were produced. The factory is the vendor's concern. By shipping it as a framework, I had exported my own manufacturing tooling as the product and left the actual product (a governed, delivered application) as an exercise for the operator.

The lockstep protocol was the architecture confessing this. Two repositories that must never drift, bound by CI choreography, is one product pretending to be two; the discipline existed because the repo boundary did, not because the problem demanded it. And "technology-agnostic" had quietly become a liability ledger: the agnosticism lived in adapters that mostly did not exist yet, which means it was a promise, while the one real Encore.ts path was a fact. Frameworks are judged on their promises. Products are judged on their facts.

## Where the factory went

In [Statecraft](https://github.com/statecrafting/statecraft), the factory is a service. It lives inside the control plane alongside the other services (tenants, fleet, governance), and its job is narrow: given a tenant and a request, stamp an application from the [enrahitu](/writing/enrahitu-the-whole-app-in-one-container) template into that tenant's own GitHub org, under that tenant's own GitHub App installation, and record every step of the run in the control plane's ledger.

The contract survived the move but changed species. The Build Specification was a technology-free document standing between process and implementation. Its successor, `template.toml`, is a versioned machine contract published by the template itself, and it comes with the enforcement the old contract never had: the factory is forbidden, by owning spec, from reaching past the contract into template internals. Not discouraged by convention; refused at the coupling gate. The old cross-repo lockstep dissolved into something stronger and simpler: one template, one contract, one consumer that is physically incapable of depending on anything else.

What I gave up is real, and I want to name it rather than spin it. The dream of "add a stack by adding an adapter" is gone; Statecraft stamps EnRaHiTu applications, full stop. But the deeper separation the old factory argued for did not die; it moved down the stack and got teeth. Process and implementation are still divided by a frozen contract. The contract is just honest now about which implementation exists, and versioned so the division can evolve without breaking either side.

## Stamping is a governed run, not a code generator

The other thing that changed is what a "run" produces. A code generator's output is code. This factory's output is code *plus custody*: the stamp is recorded as a run in the control plane, the produced repository belongs to the tenant from its first commit (their org, their GitHub App installation, their keys), and the run-side paperwork is built for adversarial verification. The certificate toolkit carried over from the old world intact: tenant-emit builds a signed governance certificate from a finished run directory, and tenant-tail re-verifies that paperwork offline, identity-free, with zero trust in the producer. The factory can vouch for a run, and the tenant can check the vouching without believing a word the factory says.

And because a stamped application knows exactly which template version it came from, the relationship between the copy and the original is a contract rather than a divergence. Template upgrades become a governed verb (exposed through [statecraft-cli](/writing/one-binary-two-faces)) instead of the slow fork-rot that kills every cloned starter kit. The factory does not just create applications; it stays accountable to them across versions.

## The boundary I had to find twice

The lesson generalizes past factories, which is why it earns an essay. The old design drew the product boundary around the *mechanism*, because the mechanism was the interesting engineering. The new design draws it around the *obligation*: what a tenant actually needs to receive, hold, and verify. Mechanisms make satisfying frameworks and confusing products. Obligations make boring services that people can actually buy.

A factory, it turns out, is a capability of a control plane, the way a printing press is a capability of a publisher. I spent a year shipping the press. The publisher works better.
